Your Navigation Stack

Reliable navigation in Africa needs redundancy — no single device or app is enough. The job is spread across a dashboard GPS, two phones, an iPad, a satellite tracker and a paper atlas, each with different strengths. Knowing what each does well, and where it fails, is what keeps you found.

The layered approach: Plan in Gaia — at gaiagps.com on the computer or on the iPad — and the route reaches both phones and the iPad. Who Leads, Who Backs Up

Every critical tool needs four answers: what you use first, what you use second, what you fall back on, and what is left when the electronics are gone. Decide it at home, not at the roadside.

TierWhat you useTakes over when
PrimaryGarmin Nuvi with T4A maps on the dash, plus Google Maps turn-by-turn on the phone where it knows the roadNormal driving.
SecondaryOsmAnd on the phone, checked against the T4A Guide and Gaia's satellite layerOff the tar, or when the primary is wrong or lost, or the vehicle's 12V has failed.
ContingencyThe other iPhone or the iPad, carrying the same apps and the same offline mapsThe first phone is flat, broken, lost or stolen.
EmergencyPaper atlas, with a position read off the inReach Mini 2 and the last position written in the notebookNo working phone, tablet or Garmin.

The contingency tier is only real if the maps are on it. A second device with no offline maps is not a backup. Each device downloads its own maps; no cloud copies them across. Load and test all three together (Section 3). OsmAnd and Gaia both run on the iPad, and every app works in flight mode (owner, 18 Oct 2026).

The iPad is the weak link for 2028–2030. The 7th-generation iPad cannot go past iPadOS 18 (EveryMac, checked 18 Oct 2026). Today OsmAnd needs iPadOS 15 or later and Gaia iPadOS 16 or later (App Store listings, checked 18 Oct 2026), so both still install. When an app raises its minimum above iPadOS 18, this iPad stops getting its updates. Check each app's minimum in the App Store before every trip.

What would change this: if either app's minimum passes iPadOS 18, the iPad stops being a full contingency navigator and becomes a spare screen for whatever still runs on it.

Check Before You Follow

A navigator is a suggestion, not an instruction. Every one of these tools is sometimes wrong, and it is wrong with complete confidence.

1. Zoom out before you set off. Look at the whole route. Does the direction and the distance agree with the paper atlas and with what you planned last night?

2. Confirm what matters in a second source. A border post, a booked camp, the only fuel for the day: find it in the T4A Guide or OsmAnd as well, and check both put it in the same place.

3. Question a surprise turn. If the device wants you off the main road and you did not expect it, stop and zoom out before you turn.

4. After a wrong turn, stop and re-plan. Do not let it re-route you blind. Check fuel, daylight and the track ahead before you choose between going on and going back.

5. Local advice outranks the screen. If someone who lives there says do not take that road, do not take it.

Gaia GPS (Premium)

Primary role: Route planning, track recording and a satellite view. Its turn-by-turn does not work well for us: plan the route in Gaia, then drive it with the Garmin or OsmAnd. Plan at gaiagps.com on the computer or on the iPad; routes, waypoints and tracks sync to the phones and the iPad when they next have signal.

Map layers: Use the Gaia Overland layer for off-road work — road surfaces and places to stop, drawn for overlanding. Switch on satellite to check that a track on the map really exists on the ground before you commit to it.

Downloads: Offline maps need an active Premium membership. Download the route corridor before you leave signal. Satellite imagery is capped at 10,000 tiles per download and 160,000 in total (Gadget Hacks, citing Gaia's help pages — a secondary report, not re-read at Gaia), so download satellite for the doubtful stretches, in pieces. Download at zoom level 15 or more for off-road areas.

Key features for overlanding: Drop waypoints at key points (turn-offs, water, good camps). Import GPX tracks from other overlanders, and export a planned route as GPX for OsmAnd. Measure distances. Show coordinates in decimal degrees (Section 6).

Record the track twice. Apps stop recording without telling you. Keep Gaia recording on the phone and inReach tracking running as the independent second record, and check at every stop that Gaia is still recording. We have not recorded a track in Gaia yet as at 4 October 2026 — the first one is tracked as OI-114.

OsmAnd (Pro)

Primary role: Turn-by-turn navigation on the phone once you leave the tar, with no signal needed. It works from OpenStreetMap, the community map that is usually the best record of African rural roads and tracks. It is the phone's answer when the Garmin is down, and the second opinion when you want to check the Garmin.

Map style: Configure map → Styles for offline maps → Offroad. It brings tracks and surfaces forward.

Routing: Use the car profile. Off the tar, make sure "avoid unpaved roads" is switched off: an avoided road type is left out of every route completely (OsmAnd's routing guide), so with it on, OsmAnd will not route you onto gravel at all. To drive a route planned in Gaia, open the GPX file in OsmAnd, tap the track and choose Navigate.

Downloads: Pro allows every map we need. Maps come one country (or region) at a time and must be on each device before you leave coverage. Test at home: switch to flight mode and ask it for a route.

OsmAnd Cloud (Pro): Keeps settings, profiles, favourites, tracks and avoided roads the same on all three devices. It does not copy the maps, and on the iPhones and the iPad it does not back up by itself: tap Sync now after any change (OsmAnd Cloud page; automatic backup is Android only).

Pro extras: A weather forecast on the map and 3D relief. Hourly map updates matter little to us — few people edit OpenStreetMap in the remote places we drive.

Limits: A track on the map tells you someone once drove it, not that it is open, dry or legal today. Where the country profile warns of landmines, stay on established routes whatever the map shows.

Tracks4Africa Guide App

Primary role: "Where is there a campsite / fuel station / mechanic near me?" This is the Africa-specific places database — no other app comes close for overland information. Toggle categories on and off (camping, fuel, lodging, services, parks) to see what's nearby. Tap a pin for details, contacts, facilities. It shows your position on the map but gives no turn-by-turn directions: use it for planning and discovery, not for driving.

The app is being replaced. Tracks4Africa is moving Guide App users to a new Tracks4Africa app sold by subscription. Anyone who bought Guide App maps and guides gets a free Lifetime Base subscription once they link those purchases to a Tracks4Africa account. Support for the old app on Android ended on 1 June 2026; the date for iOS was still to be announced (Tracks4Africa help page, updated 29 Jul 2026, checked 18 Oct 2026).

To keep what we paid for: open the Guide App while online, sign in with the Tracks4Africa account (top-left menu → Login), then sign in to the new app when it comes. Tracked as OI-120.

Updates: You carry the full Africa set of guides and maps. Check for updated versions over Wi-Fi before each trip.

Google Maps (Offline Areas)

Primary role: Tar road routing between towns with turn-by-turn directions and ETA. Download offline areas for each major route segment before losing cell coverage. Each offline area covers a region roughly 100 × 100 km at useful detail. Download multiple areas to cover your planned route.

Limitation: Offline areas expire after ~30 days. You'll need to re-download when you next have WiFi. Google Maps has almost no data on dirt tracks, 4×4 routes, or campsites — it will try to route you on tar even when a direct dirt road exists. Never use Google Maps as your only navigation tool off tar.

Garmin Nuvi (T4A Maps)

Primary role: Always-on dashboard navigation with T4A routing. Permanently mounted on the windscreen and wired into the 12V socket — it has essentially no internal battery (~30 seconds). It uses its own GPS chipset (doesn't drain your phone), and the T4A maps provide Africa-specific routing including gravel roads and 4×4 tracks that Google Maps doesn't know about. If the vehicle's 12V cuts out, the Nuvi dies immediately — your phone becomes primary.

Map updates: Tracks4Africa sells the Garmin maps two ways: as a download with a 1-year or 5-year update subscription (new versions on 1 March, 1 June, 1 September and 1 December; v26.09 current), or on a preloaded micro-SD card (v26.05, May 2026) that comes with a free upgrade to the next mapset once the card is registered (Tracks4Africa shop and blog, checked 18 Oct 2026). Ours is the download with an update subscription (owner, 18 Oct 2026): before each trip, load the latest version from the computer with Garmin's map tools.

Backup role: If a phone is lost, stolen, or broken, the Garmin keeps you navigating — as long as the vehicle's 12V is running. Because the Nuvi has no meaningful internal battery, the phones are actually the backup if the vehicle electrics fail. This is why both matter.

iOverlander (Planning)

Primary role: Planning, at camp or wherever you have signal. Other travellers' reports on wild camps, border crossings, fuel, water and mechanics. Use it alongside the T4A Guide: T4A for established places, iOverlander for what a traveller found last month.

How to use it: Read the date on every report before you trust it. When you have chosen a place, copy its coordinates into OsmAnd or Gaia, or enter them in the Garmin — iOverlander does not navigate, it hands the place to another app.

Limits: We use it online only. Offline use needs the newer iOverlander 2 app and a paid plan; whether that is worth having is undecided (OI-114). Until then, do the planning while you have signal and save the places you want. A spot everyone can see on the app gets heavy use: leave it cleaner than you found it (R17 no-trace doctrine).

Section 3

Offline Map Preparation — Before Each Country

The golden rule: download everything you might need while you still have WiFi. Once you're in the bush, it's too late. Do this at every lodge, guesthouse, or café with WiFi along the route.

Pre-country download checklist:

1. T4A Guide App: The full Africa set is installed. Check for updates over Wi-Fi. Until the move to the new Tracks4Africa app is done (OI-120), make sure the app is signed in to the Tracks4Africa account.

2. Gaia GPS: Download the planned route corridor with a buffer (50+ km either side) in case you divert, at zoom level 15+ for off-road areas. Add satellite imagery for the stretches you are unsure of.

3. OsmAnd: Download the map for the country you are entering and the one after it. Confirm in flight mode that it will work out a route. Tap Sync now in OsmAnd Cloud.

4. Google Maps: Download offline areas covering your planned tar road segments. Remember — these expire in ~30 days.

5. Garmin Nuvi: Load the latest T4A map. No per-country download needed — the T4A GPS map covers all of Africa.

6. iOverlander: Plan the next country's camps, fuel and border crossing while you have signal, and save the coordinates of the places you choose into OsmAnd or Gaia. It does not work offline without a paid plan.

7. Every device: Repeat steps 1 to 4 on the iPhone 15, the iPhone 16 Pro Max and the iPad. Each device downloads its own maps. A backup with no maps on it is not a backup.

Paper maps: Carry a current paper road atlas of Southern and East Africa (T4A publishes one). Paper doesn't run out of battery, doesn't break when dropped, and gives you the big-picture strategic view that no phone screen can match. Use it for route planning at camp in the evening — mark your route, identify fuel stops, plan the next day's drive.

Section 4

Satellite Communication

Cell coverage in Africa is surprisingly good along major routes — but disappears completely in national parks, remote tracks, border regions, and vast stretches of the countries on your route. A satellite communication device is non-negotiable for a two-year trip. You carry a Garmin InReach Mini 2 for tracking and SOS, and an Inmarsat IsatPhone 2.1 for satellite voice calls.

Why we carry both. The InReach Mini 2 for its unbeatable SOS function, location tracking (so family knows where you are), and battery life. The sat phone for voice calls — when you need to actually talk to someone (a mechanic in the next town, an embassy, medical advice, your insurance company, or family in an emergency). The InReach is your passive safety net; the sat phone is your active communication tool.

Inmarsat IsatPhone 2.1 — Our Satellite Phone

Our satellite phone: Inmarsat IsatPhone 2.1. Best battery life of any satellite phone (8 hrs talk, 160 hrs standby). IP65 rated. Programmable Assistance button — not configured, and not an SOS for us (R43). Covers all of Africa, Europe, Asia — everywhere except extreme polar regions. The workhorse sat phone used by NGOs and aid workers across Africa.

Device details: the satellite number, the plan and the SIM details are kept in Facts §11C. R43 is the handset manual.

Doctrine: inReach Mini 2 for tracking/GPS/SOS plus the IsatPhone 2.1 for emergency voice calls. Both channels run simultaneously in a serious emergency — independent satellite networks (Iridium LEO + Inmarsat GEO). The inReach SOS reaches Garmin's rescue coordination centre; on the satphone you dial the provider yourself (method R43). R29 §7 is authoritative for the activation protocol.

OUTSTANDING: the Assistance-button decision and a test-dial of the provider numbers from the handset (items 6–10 below, tracked as OI-3 / RA-10). Registering the satphone with Garmin's rescue centre was dropped (owner decision, 8 August 2026).

Operational notes: For emergency-only use the airtime cost is small; the prepaid units, their expiry and when to top up are in R43 §5. Two caveats: being geostationary, the Inmarsat signal can be blocked in deep ravines or steep valleys where the Iridium-based inReach would still connect; and a handful of countries restrict sat-phone import or use, so check the rule at each border.

InReach Limitations You Must Know

Cannot contact toll-free numbers. The InReach (and Iridium network generally) cannot message or call 0800, 1-800, or similar toll-free numbers. This means you cannot use the InReach to contact most insurance emergency lines, airline helpdesks, or embassy toll-free numbers. You need the sat phone or a local SIM for those calls. Pre-save the direct-dial (non-toll-free) numbers for your insurance, embassy contacts, and medical evacuation provider.

Two-way messaging only — no voice. The InReach sends and receives short text messages via satellite. It cannot make voice calls. In a genuine emergency where you need to explain a complex situation, give medical details, or coordinate with local authorities, text is not enough. That's why the sat phone is essential alongside the InReach.

InReach SOS — Who Pays for the Rescue?

The InReach subscription includes SOS coordination — but NOT rescue costs. When you press the SOS button, Garmin Response (formerly GEOS) coordinates the rescue: they contact local emergency services, relay your position, and manage communication. This coordination is included in your InReach subscription. However, the actual cost of the rescue — helicopter, ambulance, search teams, medical evacuation — is NOT covered by the subscription.

Garmin SAR Insurance (Search and Rescue) reimburses up to USD 100,000 in rescue costs. Currently ~USD 40/year but only available for US and Canada. Not applicable to your trip.

What you need instead: Your travel insurance must explicitly include emergency evacuation and repatriation cover — ideally USD 500,000+. Confirm it covers helicopter evacuation, air ambulance, and medical repatriation from every country on your route. Also confirm it covers evacuation triggered by an InReach SOS (some policies only cover evacuations arranged through their own emergency line). Overwatch & Rescue (OxR) at ~USD 80/year is an alternative that works internationally and covers rescue costs directly — worth investigating as a supplement to your travel insurance.

Bottom line: The InReach SOS gets you found and gets help dispatched. Your travel insurance pays for the rescue itself. Without proper evacuation cover, an SOS in a remote African country could result in a USD 20,000–50,000 bill for a helicopter evacuation.

Starlink & High-Bandwidth Data (2026)

Read this first: Starlink is a data tool, not a safety device. It has no emergency SOS, needs mains-or-DC power, a clear sky and a few minutes to acquire — it cannot replace the InReach and sat phone, which remain your non-negotiable emergency layer. What it adds to a two-year trip is genuine broadband in the middle of nowhere: route and weather planning, downloading map and software updates, video calls home, banking, and working remotely. A comfort-and-logistics upgrade, not a lifeline.

Since this guide was first written, Starlink has gone from absent to widely available across the region — but availability is fragmented by country and the regulatory picture keeps moving. As of 2026, the position on your route is roughly:

Two structural catches for a two-year trip.

1. The home-base problem. Because South Africa is unlicensed, you cannot legally buy or register a kit at home. Plan to acquire and activate it in a country where it is licensed — Botswana is the obvious first stop on your route. A kit registered abroad and used inside SA risks seizure.

2. Roaming is restricted, and the two-month rule bites. A single kit does not safely roam the whole continent: several governments (the Central African Republic, and earlier Botswana, Zimbabwe and SA) have cracked down on unauthorised roaming kits, and Starlink’s own Roam plans cap continuous international use at about two months per trip before forcing a plan re-evaluation. For a 24-month, multi-country expedition you must actively manage the account’s country and plan as you go — this is not set-and-forget.

Starlink Plans, Hardware & Power

Plan. The mobile plan is Roam, billed month-to-month with a Standby / pause mode (~USD 5/month) so you only pay for active months. In South African pricing the Roam package runs about ZAR 900/month for 100 GB of high-speed data, then throttles to under 1 Mbps; globally the tiers sit around USD 50 (regional, ~50–100 GB priority) to USD 165–200 (unlimited / global). "Unlimited" is marketing — every tier has a priority-data allowance, after which you are deprioritised. Budget data like fuel: one 4K film is ~14 GB, so 100 GB is comfortable for comms, planning and the odd call, not for streaming. Prices change often — verify at purchase.

Hardware. The Starlink Mini is the right unit for overlanding: portable, no separate router, recently dropped to roughly USD 200–600 depending on market and promotion. It points manually (no motor), has no internal battery, and — being a small dish — suffers rain fade more than the full-size unit. Keep firmware current.

Power — and how it lands on the LC76 (cross-ref R26). The Mini draws about 20–40 W in use (~15 W idle) on 12–48 V DC, but startup briefly spikes toward 100 W. Two rules for your setup:

Run it on DC, not through the inverter. The MultiPlus gives you a clean AC path if you ever need one, but an inverter’s idle draw plus conversion losses can roughly double consumption for a 20 W device — a poor trade for a load this small. Power the Mini directly from the 12 V house system via its DC input and keep the MultiPlus off (or charger-only) while it runs.

On the LiFePO4 system, run direct from 12 V — the step-up converter is no longer assumed necessary. The old advice to fit a 12 V → 30 V step-up existed because a lead-acid bank sagging below ~11.5 V under load would trip the Mini into a reboot loop on its startup spike. The Enertec 210Ah bank holds a flat ~13 V under load across most of its discharge curve, so that failure mode is largely gone. Test the Mini direct from a 12 V house outlet first; only add a step-up if long cable runs introduce enough voltage drop to cause startup resets — treat it as a fix for a measured problem, not default kit. Endurance is no longer the constraint it was: roughly 80–100 hours of Mini use from the 210Ah bank at 80% usable, before counting any solar input — but it still dies the instant the bank does, so budget power alongside data.

Phone-Based Satellite — Emerging Backup Only

Recent phones can now reach satellites directly: iPhone 14 and later use Globalstar for Emergency SOS and, on iOS 18, Messages-via-satellite and roadside help; Pixel 9 / 10 do satellite messaging via Skylo. Worth knowing your handset has it — but for this route in 2026 the coverage is geographically limited and carrier-dependent, far slower than cellular, and not something to depend on. Treat it as a tertiary fallback behind the InReach and sat phone, never a replacement for either.

Section 5

Cell Coverage & SIM Card Strategy

Cell coverage in Africa is better than most people expect — virtually every town of any size has 3G or 4G coverage, and major highways between towns often have signal. The gaps are in national parks, remote border regions, and the long empty stretches between population centres.

SIM Card Strategy

Buy a local SIM in every country. This is by far the cheapest way to get data and voice. A local SIM with 5–10 GB of data typically costs USD 3–10 and lasts 30 days. With a local number you can make local calls (arranging campsites, mechanics, border info) at local rates. Your iPhone must be unlocked — verify this before the trip.

Where to buy: At the border (vendors often sell SIMs right at the crossing), at the first town's mobile operator shop, or at the airport. Major operators: Vodacom/MTN (SA, Tanzania, Mozambique), Airtel (across East Africa), Safaricom (Kenya), MTN (Rwanda, Uganda, Zambia). You'll need your passport for registration in most countries — this is mandatory and they won't activate without it.

Section 6

Emergency Communication Protocol

When something goes wrong — breakdown, medical emergency, accident — clear communication can be the difference between a minor inconvenience and a serious situation. Have this protocol agreed and practiced before the trip. Cross-reference R10 (Emergency Procedures) for the broader emergency framework, and R38 §1 for the check-in SOP and escalation tree this protocol plugs into.

Step-by-Step Emergency Communication

1. Assess safety. Are you and Mary-Ann safe? Is the vehicle off the road? Is there an immediate threat? Address safety first — communication second.

2. Determine your position. Open Gaia GPS or any GPS-enabled app. Read your coordinates in decimal degrees format (e.g. -22.57890, 29.12345). This is the universal format that rescue services, embassies, and insurance companies understand. Write them down on paper in case your phone dies.

3. Choose your communication device: Cell signal available? → Use your phone. No signal? → Use the sat phone for voice. Sat phone not working or can't get a clear sky view? → Use the InReach Mini 2 to send a text message with your coordinates. Life-threatening emergency with no other option? → Press the InReach SOS button — this triggers Garmin Response (formerly GEOS), the International Emergency Response Centre (24/7), who will coordinate rescue with local authorities.

4. Make the call. State clearly: Who you are. Where you are (coordinates in decimal degrees + nearest town/landmark). What happened. What you need. How many people are involved. Any injuries.

5. Stay in position. Unless you're in immediate danger, stay with the vehicle. It's easier to find than two people walking.

Fail-Safe Device Verification — RA-10

The InReach is the layer that works when everything else is dead — but only if it is registered, subscribed, charged, and on your person. A packed device burns or sinks with the vehicle. Verified before the Karoo loop, then re-verified before every major expedition leg (the R38 §1 check-in SOP holds the matching sign-off placeholders):

1. Registration current. Garmin account emergency contacts and personal details up to date — Garmin Response works from what is on file, not what you meant to update.

2. Subscription active. Confirm the plan is live and covers the route. A lapsed subscription fails silently — the device looks normal until you need it.

3. Live test. Send a test message and confirm the anchor (R38 §1) received it — an end-to-end proof, not a battery check. Log the date.

4. Carried on the person. InReach travels in a pocket or on a belt when away from the vehicle, and within arm's reach when driving — never buried in a drawer system.

5. Named owner. One crew member owns charge, carriage, and currency. Shared responsibility is no responsibility.

Satellite phone verification (IsatPhone 2.1 — OI-3 / RA-10):

6. Emergency method known. The satphone is not registered with Garmin's rescue centre (owner decision, 8 August 2026). In an emergency you dial the provider yourself — numbers in R29 §6, handset steps in R43. The Assistance button is not configured; whether to set it, and to which number, is still open (OI-3).

7. SIM & airtime live. Prepaid balance or postpaid plan confirmed active; a dead SIM fails as silently as a lapsed InReach plan. Re-verify after every top-up or plan change.

8. Test call. Make a short test call from the handset after any SIM or plan change. Log the date alongside the InReach live-test date.

9. Carried accessibly. The handset lives where a passenger can reach it in a damaged vehicle — not buried in the drawer system. Antenna use requires open sky toward the equator; know that before the emergency.

10. Medevac numbers TEST-DIALLED from this handset. Dial each international medevac number in the R29 §6 master set from the satphone itself and confirm a human answers, then log the date. This is not pedantry: the numbers are provider-published but were not voice-tested when they were written into the library, and a number that has never been dialled on the equipment that will dial it is an assumption, not a control. Until this item is signed off, AMREF is the only medevac number with a confirmed satellite route. Re-test after any SIM, plan or handset change.

Emergency Contacts — Pre-Load These

Travel insurance emergency line: Save both the local and international number. Test that it works from your sat phone before the trip.

SA Embassy contacts: For each country on your route, save the SA embassy/consulate phone number and physical address. Cross-reference R15 (Border Crossings) country cards.

Medical evacuation: Your insurance provider's emergency evacuation coordinator, plus the medevac master set — AMREF +254 730 811 811 / +254 709 962 811 (East Africa), ER24 international dispatch +27 10 205 3000 and aeromedical / flight desk +27 10 205 3588. Save the international forms, not the SA short-dial codes: 082 911 and 084 124 reach the same services from an in-country mobile network but cannot be routed by Iridium or Inmarsat and will fail from the satphone. These numbers are OWNED by R29 §6 — if this page ever disagrees with it, R29 is right. The crew’s own cover — Neil’s Genesis (0861 436 374), Mary-Ann’s Bonitas/AZOZA SOS (0860 555 505) and Cross Country's in-country vehicle line (0800 005 688) — are SA-network-only codes for the cellphone tier only and will not route from a satphone; the satphone-dialable equivalents (ER24 +27 10 205 3000, AZOZA +27 11 991 8174; Cross Country +27 11 966 5004 for the vehicle) are carried in R29 §6 and the R10 top block.

Vehicle recovery: Save AA of SA's international number. In practice, local recovery is arranged through your insurance or by calling a local contact — but having the AA number gives you a starting point.

Family contact: One designated person at home who knows your route, has your insurance details, and can coordinate on your behalf if you're incapacitated. Share your InReach tracking link with them.

Coordinate Format Reference

Always use decimal degrees for emergency communication. Example: -22.578901, 29.123456 (negative = south of equator, positive = east of Greenwich). This format is unambiguous — rescue services, Google Maps, and WhatsApp location sharing all understand it. Avoid degrees-minutes-seconds (DMS) for emergencies — it's easy to mis-read or mis-transcribe. Set all your navigation apps to display decimal degrees as the primary format.

Section 7

Device Power Management

Your navigation and communication devices are useless with flat batteries. On a two-year trip, power discipline is a daily habit.

iPhone: The biggest power consumer. GPS + screen + navigation app = flat in 3–4 hours. Plug into the 12V charger whenever driving. Use the Garmin Nuvi as primary dashboard navigation to preserve phone battery. Turn off WiFi, Bluetooth, and background app refresh when not needed. Enable low-power mode when off the charger. Carry a 20,000 mAh power bank as backup — charges the iPhone 4–5 times. The iPad follows the same rules; it charges by Lightning, the two iPhones by USB-C.

Garmin Nuvi: Permanently wired to 12V — has essentially no internal battery (~30 seconds). If the engine is running, the Nuvi is running. If the vehicle electrics fail, the Nuvi dies. This means your iPhone (with its own battery and power bank) is your true emergency backup, not the Nuvi.

Sat phone (IsatPhone 2.1): Outstanding standby life (160 hours). Keep it off unless you need to make a call or are expecting one. Check it once daily. Charge it off the house bank with its car charger, or from USB (R43 §2).

InReach Mini 2: In 10-minute tracking mode, battery lasts ~3.5 days. In 30-minute tracking mode, ~7 days. In expedition mode (1-hour intervals), ~14 days. Charge via USB when driving. The InReach's power efficiency is one of its biggest advantages over a sat phone for always-on tracking.

Charging: All devices charge via USB. The vehicle's 12V system (the LiFePO4 house battery) feeds a multi-port USB charger on the dashboard. Carry two chargers (one in the cab, one at camp) and a quality multi-cable. Cross-reference R5 (Battery Training) for auxiliary system details.
