1. Introduction & Safety Principles

❌ Recovery Safety — Read First
Recovery operations are high-risk. Equipment failure can cause serious injury or death.

❌ NEVER use tow balls for recovery — they become lethal projectiles

❌ NEVER stand in the recovery line (front or rear of vehicles)

❌ NEVER use damaged, frayed, or kinked equipment

❌ NEVER exceed equipment working load limits

❌ NEVER rush — assess the situation completely first

✓ ALWAYS use rated recovery points (ARB or equivalent)

✓ ALWAYS lay a damper over the line — the heavy ropes, a bag or a blanket

✓ ALWAYS keep bystanders at least 1.5× rope length away

✓ ALWAYS wear gloves when handling ropes and cables

🎯 Recovery Decision Hierarchy
Work through this list in order. Stop at the first option that works.
1. Self-recovery without equipment — reverse out, different line
2. Reduce tyre pressure — increases footprint, improves traction
3. Use recovery boards — traction under wheels
4. Dig out / clear obstacles — shovel, pick, jack base plate
5. Vehicle-to-vehicle recovery — the other vehicle's kinetic rope, or a tow with our tow strap
6. Winch recovery — solo or assisted
7. Call for professional recovery
🧠 Pre-Recovery Risk Assessment
Before any recovery attempt, assess these factors.

Terrain stability: Is the ground getting softer? Rising water?

Vehicle position: Level, tilted, risk of rollover?

Anchor points: What's available? Trees, rocks, buried spare?

Equipment condition: Inspect all gear before use

Weather: Changing conditions? Approaching darkness?

Personnel: Who knows recovery procedures? Who operates controls?

If in doubt — wait for help. A stuck vehicle is recoverable; injuries may not be.

🔍 Why You're Stuck — Lift and Pull
Every bogging is a shortage of lift, of pull, or both.

Lift gets the wheels back onto something firm — dig, jack, boards, lower pressure, offload. Pull moves the vehicle once they are — its own drive, people pushing, another vehicle, the winch. Work out which you are short of before you start.

1. Stop the wheels the moment you are stuck — every extra turn digs you deeper
2. Try reversing out first, in your own tracks — the way you came in held you
3. Are the tyre pressures right for this ground? If not, fix them first (R14)
4. Is any weight on the chassis, diffs or sills instead of the wheels? Dig or jack that clear before anything else
5. Look at all four wheels and find the one that is holding you — work on that one first, then any other wheel without a clear path ahead
6. Finish the job before you try — a half-hearted dig that fails means doing it all again
7. Use what is around you: firm ground behind or ahead, a slight downslope, offloading heavy cargo

If the first attempts fail, stop, have a drink, and work out why before trying again.

2. Terrain-Specific Recovery

🏜️ Sand Recovery
Kalahari, Namib, coastal dunes. The 1HZ's torque works well at low RPM but lacks power for steep dunes.

Tyre pressures: R14, Sand (soft, deep) row

Recovery procedure:

1. Stop immediately when wheels spin — don't dig deeper
2. Drop to R14's soft-sand pressures; go to its dune row only if still sinking — never below it
3. Clear sand from around tyres, underneath diffs
4. Place recovery boards in front of driven wheels
5. Select low range, diff-lock engaged
6. Drive smoothly onto boards, maintain momentum
7. Once clear, stop and retrieve boards

1HZ Note: Without turbo boost at low RPM, the naturally aspirated feel requires momentum planning. Don't expect to power through — use technique.

🌧️ Mud Recovery
Black cotton soil, vleis, wet tracks. Can be deceptively deep and grab axles.

Tyre pressures: R14, Mud row

Before entering:

• Gauge depth with a stick — look for submerged ruts, obstacles

• Check exit route — is it worse than entry?

Recovery procedure:

1. Engage low range, diff-lock, before getting stuck
2. If stuck, DON'T spin wheels — worsens situation
3. Try rocking: forward/reverse in quick succession
4. Clear mud from around tyres, under chassis
5. Use boards, branches, rocks under wheels for traction
6. If deep, jack the vehicle on a base plate (ARB hydraulic or bottle jack), pack material underneath

⚠️ Mud suction can hold 3+ tonnes. Winch slowly, check for movement, don't shock-load.

🪨 Rock & Technical Recovery
Hi-centering, wheel wedging, body contact. The LC76's solid axles articulate well but watch for body damage.

Tyre pressures: R14, Rocky terrain (crawl) row

Common situations:

Hi-centered: Vehicle resting on chassis/diff, wheels off ground

• Use the ARB hydraulic or bottle jack on a base plate to raise the vehicle

• Stack rocks/boards under lifted wheel

• Lower jack, drive off obstacle

Wedged wheel: Tyre trapped between rocks

• DON'T force — will damage tyre sidewall

• Jack up, clear rocks, lower back

Spotting protocol:

• Spotter walks ahead, maintains eye contact

• Hand signals: stop, go slow, left, right, reverse

• Driver follows spotter's commands exactly

⛰️ Steep Hill Recovery
Mountain passes, dongas. Risk of rollover if recovery angle is wrong.

Stalled on ascent:

1. Hold hard on the footbrake (no servo with the engine off). Never press the clutch to stop a stall.
2. Select low-range reverse, clutch fully out
3. Ease off the brake, then turn the key — the 76 cranks in gear and backs down under engine braking
4. Wheels straight, both hands on the wheel — turning causes rollover. Full procedure: R28 §8

Failed descent (too steep/slippery):

1. Stop, engage handbrake
2. Winch to anchor point UPHILL
3. Control descent with winch tension + brakes

⚠️ NEVER turn sideways on a slope. If sliding sideways, straighten wheels and try to regain forward/backward control.

The fitted 9,500 lb winch (~4,309 kg) is about 1.3 times — short of that rule. It is kept (owner ruling, 4 Nov 2026), so double-line sooner: whenever the load table below gives more than about two-thirds of the rating for the layer you are on.

Electrical doctrine while winching: the winch is wired crank-direct — it draws off the Enertec 650B-BLS crank battery (several hundred amps under load), not the LiFePO4 house bank (R26 §1 wiring note). ALWAYS winch with the engine running above idle so the alternator supports the crank battery (the T-Max figure for rpm and current is being confirmed — OI-116) — hold the idle up with the throttle knob, clockwise to raise it and anticlockwise to return it, and never use the knob while the vehicle is moving (owner’s manual p. 118); after a long pull, leave the engine running so the battery recovers before you switch off; a stalled pull on a dead engine can flatten the crank battery in minutes and leave you stuck AND unable to start. Winch in bursts with cooling pauses — winch motors are short-duty-cycle devices, and the motor runs hotter as the battery voltage falls. Stop periodically to let it cool and the battery recover; keep the voltage above about 10 V. The maker's duty cycle governs once in hand (OI-116). The M-series switch and house electronics play no part in winching.

Pull rating drops with every layer of rope on the drum: the rated pull is on the first layer; each layer after that gives roughly 10% less. For a hard pull, spool out until you are on the bottom layer, or double-line. The T-Max chart governs once in hand (OI-116).

🌳 Anchor Selection
A failed anchor can become a projectile. Always verify before loading.

Trees:

• A live, sound tree — the thicker the better; if in doubt, use two trees (§4, Rigging 6) or another anchor

• Use tree protector strap — wide, no metal

• Wrap LOW on trunk (strongest point)

• Avoid dead trees, leaning trees, shallow-rooted species

Rocks/Boulders:

• Must be solidly embedded, not sitting on surface

• Wrap strap around, not hooked over edge

Buried spare wheel (land anchor):

• Dig a hole at least as deep as the tyre is tall, with a sloping slit trench toward the vehicle for the strap; keep the side facing the pull vertical and solid

• Bury wheel flat, attach strap through centre

• Pack firmly, wet if possible

Another vehicle:

• Must be secure — brakes on, wheels chocked

• Connect to rated recovery points ONLY

⚙️ Winching Setup Procedure
Proper setup prevents equipment damage and injury.
1. Stuck vehicle: engine running, low range, driver at the wheel. Bonnet up to shield the windscreen. One person only works the winch control
2. Disengage winch clutch (free-spool mode)
3. Walk rope to anchor — don't drive over it
4. Wrap tree protector low on tree trunk
5. Connect shackle through strap eyes (pin seated, then backed off half a turn)
6. Connect winch hook or soft shackle to strap shackle
7. Engage winch clutch, take up slack SLOWLY
8. Lay a damper over the rope — two of the heavy ropes, or a bag or blanket — before it takes load. Once the rope is tight, leave the damper alone; slack off first to move it
9. Clear ALL personnel to 1.5× rope length
10. Operator: signal ready, begin winching in short pulls. Stuck vehicle drives gently in low-range second, matching the winch speed — no wheelspin; if it lurches and jerks the line, go to neutral and let the winch work

⚠️ Operator rules: hold the hook by its strap and keep fingers out of the hook and the fairlead; never step over a rope under load — walk round; never free the winch clutch while the rope is under load; keep the remote lead clear of the drum and pass it through the window, not the door; unplug the remote when finished.

How the parts join up — drawings in §4, Rigging 2, 4, 5, 6 and 7. Pulling round a corner through a ring on a tree adds no pull, and the tree carries up to twice the pull: §4, Rigging 7.

🔄 Single vs Double Line Pull
Double-line through the recovery ring nearly doubles pulling power and halves speed.

Single line:

• Direct connection, full speed

• Use when pull required ≤ winch capacity

Double line (recovery ring):

• Attach the ring to the anchor with the 26 t soft shackle

• Run the winch rope round the ring, back to the front bridle (§4, Rigging 3)

• Effective pull = 2× winch rating (minus friction) — and the ring and its soft shackle carry that load, about 8,600 kg. The extension rope only ever on the return run: see §4, Rigging 5

• Speed = 50% of single line

• Required for heavy mud suction, steep inclines

When to double-line: use the load table below.

📐 How Hard Is the Pull? — Load Table
Rough pull needed, so you know before you start whether to double-line.

Method: pull ≈ (slope in degrees ÷ 60 × loaded mass) + (ground share × loaded mass). Ground shares are averages from winch makers: pavement 2–4%, grass 8–14%, hard-packed sand 10–17%, wet sand 15–20%, gravel 10–20%, soft dry sand 25–30%, shallow mud 30–35%, deep mud 40–60%, deep clay mud 50–70%. 🔗 Rigging 1 — Where to Attach
Four rated recovery points: two at the front, two at the back. Nothing else on the vehicle takes a recovery load.
winch ropeextension ropetree protectorbridle (yellow sling)kinetic rope (other vehicle)tow strapringsoft shackleusenever
  1. Front recovery points, left and right, just below the ARB bar. They take the red pair of bow shackles, and for a snatch, a double-line pull or a tow, a yellow sling across both as a bridle (Rigging 3).
  2. Rear recovery points — the two tabs on the GobiX bumper, inboard of the tail lights. They take the galvanised pair (blue pins) and the second yellow sling when you are pulled out backwards or are the tug.
  3. Tow ball and tow bar — never. Made for a trailer, not a recovery: a sheared ball becomes a projectile (§5).
  4. Never the tie rod, drag link, steering arm, axle, bull-bar uprights or the swing arms — they are not rated, and steering parts bend (§7).

The winch rope leaves through the T-Max fairlead in the middle of the bar; it is the pulling end, not an attachment point for another vehicle.

The four points are treated as rated recovery points by the owner's ruling (4 Nov 2026). The makers' load ratings for them are not on record.

🔗 Rigging 2 — The Shackles and How They Go On
Four bow shackles and the soft shackles. Every connection to the vehicle is one of them — never a hook over a bar, never a knot.
  1. Bow shackles — 4, all 3/4", 4.75 t working load (stamped on the bow). The red pair lives on the front points, the galvanised pair on the rear points or lent to the other vehicle. Pin through the recovery point, bow towards the load; screw the pin in until it seats, then back it off half a turn so it does not seize under load.
  2. The sling, strap or rope eye sits in the round of the bow, never on the pin.
  3. The pull runs in a straight line from the point — no twist, no side-load on the shackle.
  4. Soft shackles — 4: two MuscleLift ½" × 6" (18,140 kg break strength each), a Rusty 4x4 18 t, and the Rusty 26 t, which is kept for the ring on the anchor, where the load is highest (Rigging 5, 7). They go at the far end — the tree protector, the ring — where a broken metal shackle would fly back at you. Through the loop and the eye, then close the loop over its knot. On the vehicle only through a hole with a smooth, rounded edge.
🔗 Rigging 3 — The Bridle
A bridle spreads the pull over both recovery points and keeps it central. Use it for a snatch, a double-line pull and a tow.
  1. Both recovery points, front or rear.
  2. A bow shackle on each point — the red pair at the front, the galvanised pair at the back. The sling's end goes in the round of the bow — never sling straight through the bare hole.
  3. A yellow round sling (Giant, 3,000 kg working load, 2.5 m, rated ×1.4 as two legs up to 45°). Thread it through the rope's eye first, then put one end on each shackle. The eye slides along the sling and shares the load between the two points by itself — no shackle at the centre.
  4. The rope, the winch return line or the tow strap runs straight out from the centre.
  5. Bridle already on? Then a soft shackle round both strands of the sling at its centre and through the rope's eye does the same job. Never a bow shackle at the centre: no metal between rope and sling.

Keep the V narrow: each leg no more than 45° off the line of the pull — the sling's label rates it for that angle. Past 60° the legs carry more than the pull itself. If the V is too wide, the sling is too short for these points.

Two yellow slings are carried: one for the front bridle, one for the rear or a second anchor. The purple 1 t sling stays at home (owner, 3 Nov 2026). Keep the black sleeve where a sling touches the bar or a shackle.

🔗 Rigging 4 — Winch to a Tree
Your own winch, single line, straight out of the fairlead — no bridle. The procedure is §3, Winching Setup; this shows how the parts join up.
  1. Winch rope out through the fairlead — free-spool to pay out, walk it, never drive over it. Keep at least 10 wraps on the drum.
  2. Damper — two of the heavy ropes laid over the winch rope about halfway, before the rope takes load. Once it is tight, leave the damper alone.
  3. A soft shackle through both eyes of the tree protector; the winch hook goes on the soft shackle. Never wrap the winch rope round the tree and hook it back on itself.
  4. Tree protector (Desert Products, 3.5 m, 10,000 kg break strength) wrapped low on a live, sound trunk (§3, Anchor Selection). For a trunk too thick for it, the second yellow sling, sleeve against the bark.
  5. Winch extension rope (Ironman 4x4, 20 m, 9,500 kg break strength) when the tree is out of reach: one eye on the soft shackle at the protector,
  6. the winch hook straight into its other eye — hook to eye, no shackle between the ropes. Still short? The green tow strap (7.5 m) adds on, joined eye through eye (Rigging 9). Keep the winch rope as short as the job allows: the first layer pulls hardest.
🔗 Rigging 5 — Double Line Through the Ring
When the load table in §3 says the pull is more than about two-thirds of the winch rating, or the vehicle is bellied or in suction mud.
  1. Recovery ring (Rusty 4x4, 15,000 kg working load, synthetic rope 7–20 mm) on the anchor. Lay the winch rope round its groove.
  2. The 26 t soft shackle through the ring's centre and both eyes of the tree protector — never a bow shackle here: this point carries about twice the winch pull, about 8,600 kg at full first-layer pull.
  3. The rope comes back to the front bridle — the yellow sling on the red pair (Rigging 3); the winch hook takes the sling at its centre, both strands. The return pull stays central, beside the fairlead.
  4. Heavy ropes over the outgoing run;
  5. and over the return run. Both runs are loaded.
  6. The tree now carries about twice the winch pull — choose it accordingly.
  7. Tree too far? The extension rope goes on the return run only: the winch hook into one of its eyes, its other eye on the bridle. There it carries one times the pull, about 4,300 kg.

⚠️ Never put the extension rope between the ring (or block) and the tree. That stretch carries about twice the winch pull — about 8,600 kg — against the extension rope's 9,500 kg break strength.

A ring has more friction than a block, so the pull is somewhat less than double. The ring is for synthetic rope only; keep the rope clean, as grit sliding round the groove wears it. Pull in short bursts and stop as soon as the winch slows hard or stalls.

🔗 Rigging 6 — No Tree Straight Ahead: Two-Tree Anchor
Two trees either side of the line make one anchor in line with the pull.
  1. Trees close together: the tree protector round one tree, low on the trunk;
  2. the second yellow sling round the other, sleeve against the bark;
  3. all the eyes on one soft shackle, and the winch hook on that. The join cannot slide, so the trees share the load only if the pull runs down the middle of the V. Keep the angle between the two legs to 60° or less: each leg at least as long as the gap between the trees. Each leg then carries about 2,500 kg at full pull.
  4. Trees further apart: the extension rope from tree to tree — one eye on the tree protector, the other on the yellow sling, each with a soft shackle.
  5. The ring rides on the extension rope, and the winch hook takes a soft shackle through its centre. The ring slides to share the load between the trees by itself, like the vehicle bridle, so up to 90° between the legs is acceptable.
  6. Heavy ropes over the winch rope, as always.
🔗 Rigging 7 — Pulling Round a Corner
When the winching vehicle cannot line up with the stuck one, a ring on a tree turns the pull. It adds no pull — and the tree carries more than the winch does.
  1. The winching vehicle — ours or the other one. Handbrake on, in gear, wheels chocked; for a hard pull, tie its back to a tree or a vehicle behind with the tow strap. Its winch and its fairlead carry one times the pull.
  2. Winch rope out to the ring and round it — one run to the anchor, one on to the stuck vehicle. Keep at least 10 wraps on the drum.
  3. Recovery ring on the anchor. It sits on the inside of the corner; the rope lies round its groove.
  4. The 26 t soft shackle through the ring's centre and both eyes of the tree protector — never a bow shackle here.
  5. The tree, tree protector low on the trunk. It is pulled into the corner, between the two runs — choose a tree that can take it.
  6. The stuck vehicle — a person at the wheel, low gear, gentle power on the signal;
  7. its bridle, with the winch hook on the sling's centre.
  8. Heavy ropes over both runs: both are loaded.

Redirect or double line? Count the ropes that get shorter as the winch turns. One rope: a redirect — the winch pulls no harder than on a single line, and the turn only loads the anchor. Two ropes: a double line (Rigging 5) — twice the pull at half the speed.

🔗 Rigging 8 — Kinetic Rope: the Other Vehicle's
No kinetic rope is carried (owner, 3 Nov 2026). A snatch is done with the other vehicle's kinetic rope; our part is the bridle and the rated points. The procedure and checklist are §5 and §8.
  1. You, stuck — on your wheels, not bellied (dig or jack first). Low gear, handbrake off, gentle power on the signal.
  2. The tug — a vehicle of similar size and weight.
  3. Your bridle: a yellow sling on the red pair if you are pulled forward, or on the galvanised pair if you are pulled out backwards. If the tug has no rated shackles or bridle of its own, lend it the other pair and the second yellow sling — never its tow ball.
  4. Their kinetic rope, laid with 2–3 m of slack, no knots or twists, within about 10° of a straight line; its eyes sit on the bridles. Read its label first: a break strength of 2 to 3 times the mass of the lighter vehicle — for us, 3,280 kg as weighed.
  5. Heavy ropes over the middle of the rope as a damper.
  6. Everyone well to the side, at least 1.5 times the rope length away — never in the red band, in front of or behind either vehicle. Rest the rope between attempts: it heats up.

⚠️ Never snatch with anything we carry. The Ironman extension rope and the green tow strap are both static — the extension rope's label says it is not a snatch strap.

🔗 Rigging 9 — Joining Two Ropes
When one rope will not reach — the extension rope and the tow strap, or yours and the other vehicle's (§5).
  1. Pass the eye of the first rope through the eye of the second,
  2. then feed the whole far end of the second rope back through the first eye, and pull the join up snug.
  3. Put a stick or a rolled cloth in the join before it takes load, so it can be undone afterwards.

Never join two ropes or straps with a shackle: if one breaks, the shackle is thrown.

🔗 Rigging 10 — Ordinary Tow
Rolling a vehicle that can steer and brake, on firm ground, with a rope or strap that does not stretch. It never snatches.
  1. Towed vehicle — someone at the wheel, ignition on so the steering does not lock, braking gently to keep the strap tight.
  2. Towing vehicle — pulls away slowly from a tight strap; no run-up.
  3. A yellow sling as the bridle across the towed vehicle's front points (red pair).
  4. The green tow strap (SecureTech pull strap, 7.5 m, 14,000 kg break strength), from the bridle to the tug's rear point.
  5. A galvanised bow shackle (or a soft shackle) on the tug's rear point.

Longer tow: the Ironman extension rope (20 m) also makes a good tow rope — the length keeps the tug off the soft patch (§5, Long-Rope and Tandem Tow). It is static, rated 9,500 kg, about 2.9 times the vehicle's weighed 3,280 kg. Keep it off the exhaust and sharp edges: its fibre softens at low heat and cuts easily. Never tow with the winch rope itself.

5. Vehicle-to-Vehicle Recovery

No kinetic rope is carried (owner, 3 Nov 2026). A snatch uses the other vehicle's kinetic rope; we supply the bridle and the rated points (§4, Rigging 8). Never snatch with the Ironman extension rope or the green tow strap — both are static.

❌ Critical: never use a tow ball. Tow balls are NOT rated recovery points — they are made for static towing loads, kinetic recovery loads exceed their rating by 500%+, and a sheared ball becomes a 3 kg projectile at 100+ km/h. Ball shearing has caused fatalities.

✓ USE ONLY: factory recovery points or aftermarket rated recovery points (ARB, KAON, etc.). On this vehicle: the two points just below the ARB bar and the two tabs on the GobiX rear bumper (§4, Rigging 1).

🔗 Kinetic Rope Recovery
Kinetic (snatch) ropes and straps stretch roughly 15–30% depending on the make, storing energy that pulls the stuck vehicle out.
1. Position recovery vehicle on firm ground, in line with stuck vehicle
2. Connect kinetic rope to RATED recovery points on both vehicles
3. Lay out the rope with about 2–3 m of slack, no knots or twists. The tug stops no closer than half the rope's length. Heavy ropes or a blanket over the rope as a damper
4. Clear all personnel to side, 1.5× rope length away
5. Stuck vehicle: low gear, handbrake OFF, driver applies gentle power on the signal — just short of wheelspin
6. Recovery vehicle: pull away in first at normal take-off speed, accelerate very gently and hold the speed constant — the rope's stretch does the work, not engine power. More speed does not mean more pull
7. Continue smoothly — rope stretches, stores energy, pulls
8. Once stuck vehicle moves, both vehicles proceed together
9. Stop on firm ground, disconnect, inspect rope. If it needs another attempt, rest the rope first — repeated pulls heat it

⚠️ Never shock-load a static rope (tow strap). Only kinetic ropes are designed for snatch recovery.

⚠️ Don't snatch a vehicle that is bellied — dig or jack the weight back onto the wheels first. The tug should be similar in size and weight to the stuck vehicle: a light tug recoils into it, a heavy one can tear it apart. Use both recovery points with a bridle — the yellow round sling, rigged as in §4, Rigging 3.

⚠️ Joining two straps or ropes: never with a shackle — if one breaks, the shackle becomes a missile. Pass the eye of one through the eye of the other, then the far end of the second back through the first eye, and pull up with a stick or rolled cloth in the join so it can be undone. Keep the pull within about 10° of straight; recovery speed no more than a fast walk.

Rope care: measure the rope's length when new and retire it from snatching once it has stretched 10% beyond that — keep it for towing. A wet rope is about 20% weaker; sunlight ages it; wash out grit after use.

🪢 Long-Rope and Tandem Tow
Two or more vehicles pulling together, under one marshaller.

A long static tow rope — 30–50 m — keeps the towing vehicle off the soft patch that stopped the first one. Our longest is the Ironman extension rope, 20 m; joined eye through eye to the green tow strap it makes 27.5 m. Longer than that, use the other vehicles' ropes.

1. One marshaller, 15–30 m ahead of the lead vehicle, where every driver can see them. Brief strangers on the signals first
2. Marshaller waves each vehicle forward in turn until the ropes are tight
3. Hand circling: everyone in gear, revs up
4. Hand drops: all clutches in together — the stuck vehicle drives too
5. Stop signal once the vehicle is clear; tug backs a metre to take the tension off before unhooking

Towing out backwards, a second marshaller relays signals to the driver facing away. Keep everyone clear of a tensioned rope.

✋ Hand Signals
Clear communication prevents accidents. Agree signals before starting.

STOP: Closed fist raised high

GO: Open palm, waving forward

SLOW DOWN: Palm down, pressing down motion

REVERSE: Beckoning toward yourself

LEFT: Point left with extended arm

RIGHT: Point right with extended arm

If radios available, use channel agreed beforehand. Keep transmissions brief and clear.

6. Self-Recovery Without Winch

📋 Recovery Boards
First line of self-recovery. Work in sand, mud, and snow.
1. Clear material from in front of tyres
2. Place boards in front of driven wheels (usually rear)
3. Angle boards down slightly into surface
4. Select low range, diff-lock if available
5. Drive SLOWLY onto boards — no wheel spin
6. Once grip established, continue smoothly off boards
7. Stop on firm ground, retrieve boards

Boards ejecting: If wheels spin on boards, they can shoot out dangerously. Never stand behind wheels during recovery.

⛏️ Dig & Fill Method
When stuck to the axles, digging is mandatory before any recovery attempt.
1. Dig channels in front of all four wheels
2. Clear material from under differentials and chassis rails
3. Fill channels with rocks, branches, boards — anything solid
4. Build a ramp up to firmer ground level
5. Reduce tyre pressure if not already done
6. Drive out slowly, using low range

This is slow, physical work but often the only option when alone without a winch.

🔧 Jack Techniques — ARB Hydraulic & Bottle
No hi-lift is carried — deliberate doctrine. Recovery pulls belong to the winch and MaxTrax; lifting belongs to the hydraulic and bottle jacks.

Lifting to insert traction:

1. Chock the opposite wheels; place the jack base plate/board — always on soft ground
2. Position the ARB hydraulic or bottle jack under the axle at a rated solid-axle jacking point (R40 §2)
3. Lift the wheel clear of the ground
4. Pack material / recovery boards under the wheel
5. Lower slowly, remove the jack, drive off

Doctrine & deferral: the pulling role a hi-lift would serve is owned by the winch and MaxTrax (this guide); the full jacking standard, bead breaking and all wheel/tyre spanner work are owned by R40 · Wheel & Tyre Field Procedures.

⚠️ A jack is a lifting tool, not a stand — never work under a vehicle held on a jack alone. When lifting with the hydraulic jack, set the bottle jack under the axle as a backup stand (R40 §2).

🔄 Rocking Technique
Uses vehicle momentum to break free from shallow bogs.
1. Select low range
2. Drive forward until wheels spin or vehicle stops
3. Immediately shift to reverse, back up until stopped
4. Shift forward again, using momentum from reverse
5. Repeat, building rhythm and rocking amplitude
6. On forward swing, continue out if traction gained

Clutch warning: Rapid forward/reverse is hard on the R151F gearbox. Don't over-rev, shift smoothly, and stop after five cycles if it is not gaining (decision tree, §8). Not in sand — rocking usually digs the vehicle in deeper there; reverse out instead (R28 §1).

7. Common Mistakes & Lessons Learned

❌ Mistakes That Cause Damage
Lessons from recovery failures — avoid these.

❌ Spinning wheels when stuck: Digs deeper, heats tyres, wastes fuel

❌ Recovering at wrong angle: Side-loads damage equipment, causes rollovers

❌ Using worn/damaged equipment: Shackle pins can fail, straps can snap

❌ Attaching to steering components: Never use track rod, tie rod, steering arm

❌ No damper on the line: If cable/rope breaks, recoil can kill

❌ Jerking with static strap: Shock loads destroy straps and connection points

❌ Standing in the line: Never between vehicles or in line with rope

✓ Best Practices
Habits that prevent problems and improve outcomes.

✓ Stop early: Don't "try a bit more" — assess before deeper

✓ Deflate first: Often solves the problem without recovery gear

✓ Dig before pulling: Reduce resistance before applying force

✓ Use a bridle: Connect to two points for straight-line pull (§4, Rigging 3)

✓ Check equipment after each use: Damage may not be obvious

✓ Practice with your gear: First use shouldn't be an emergency

✓ Travel in convoy: Vehicle-to-vehicle recovery is easier than solo

🔋 1HZ-Specific Considerations
The turbocharged 1HZ has specific characteristics affecting recovery.

Power delivery:

• Peak torque about 375 Nm at about 2,100 RPM (dyno, 26 Oct 2026)

• SAC low-boost turbo adds ~30% power over NA spec

• Intercooler reduces intake temps — important when winching

During winching:

• Winch draws several hundred amps — watch the voltmeter; pause and let the battery recover if it stays low

• Engine running above idle while winching — T-Max figure to be confirmed (OI-116)

• Watch EGT if engine loaded during recovery (climbing)

In sand:

• 1HZ lacks instant throttle response — maintain momentum

• Don't expect to power out of deep sand like V8 diesels

• Technique beats power: tyre pressure and line selection are critical

8. Quick Reference Procedures

📋 Decision Tree: Stuck in Sand
STOP immediately — don't dig deeper
↓ Deflate tyres to R14's soft-sand pressures
↓ Try reverse — is there grip?
→ YES: Reverse out, find new line
→ NO: Place recovery boards
↓ Drive onto boards — vehicle moving?
→ YES: Continue to firm ground
→ NO: Dig channels, repeat boards
↓ Still stuck? Winch or vehicle recovery
📋 Decision Tree: Stuck in Mud
STOP immediately — assess depth
↓ Engage low range, diff-lock (if not already)
↓ Try rocking forward/reverse (5 cycles max)
→ Vehicle moving? Continue out
→ No movement? Exit vehicle, assess
↓ Dig around tyres and under chassis
↓ Pack branches/rocks under wheels
↓ Place recovery boards if available
↓ Try again — moving?
→ YES: Continue to firm ground
→ NO: jack + fill, or winch/tow
